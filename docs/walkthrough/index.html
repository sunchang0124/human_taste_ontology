The taste words that no ontology had

Every glass of citrus juice in Ehime tastes different to the person next to you. That variation has no standard way to be written down. HTO is a first attempt at one, built in two days from clinical guidelines and public knowledge bases.

01 — THE GAP

Nobody had written down what taste words mean

Before building anything I checked whether this already existed. On 17 September 2026 I searched the OBO Foundry registry, all 267 ontologies, and the EBI Ontology Lookup Service. The answer was no, and the shape of the absence was sharper than expected.

There is plenty for the things you taste. FoodOn covers foods. ChEBI covers the molecules. BitterDB, FlavorDB and ChemTastesDB catalogue tastants. Gene Ontology has the perception processes. What is missing is the perception itself: the vocabulary a person uses to say what they experienced.

PATO is the ontology the biomedical world uses for qualities. It has a term for bitter. It has no term for sweet, sour, salty or umami. Astringency exists in ChEBI, but as a chemical role, which is a category error: it describes what a compound does, not what a mouth feels. Kokumi, oleogustus and mouthfeel appear nowhere at all. Aftertaste exists only in SNOMED.

02 — WHAT IT IS

Three layers, and a rule about reuse

HTO defines only what is genuinely missing. Everything else is imported: the perception processes from Gene Ontology, the anatomy from Uberon, the tastants from ChEBI, the foods from FoodOn, the taste disorders from the Human Phenotype Ontology, and, importantly, the per-modality sensitivity traits from the Ontology of Biological Attributes, which already existed and would have been wasteful to reinvent.

Quality lexicon

The descriptors themselves: five basic tastes, contested ones, bitterness subtypes, chemesthesis, temporal qualities, hedonics.

34 classes · HTO:0000100–0000162
Percept core

The tasting event, and the reified assertion that one person perceived one quality in one sample on one occasion.

27 classes · HTO:0000001–0000026
Bridges

Mappings out to PATO, NCIT, GO, ChEBI and OBA, with honest predicates rather than blanket equivalence.

30 mappings · 5 SSSOM files

External terms reused, by source ontology

56 terms imported with their real labels and definitions, fetched live from the EBI service.

The design decision that matters is that the tasting event is the centre, not the word list. A vocabulary alone cannot record that two people disagreed about the same glass. Reifying the perception can, and that is what makes person-level taste data shareable.

03 — HOW A TASTE BECOMES DATA

One row of a score sheet, one graph of triples

A taster fills in a line: participant code, sample, how sweet, how sour, how bitter, how astringent, how much they liked it, and how many minutes since the bottle was opened. The converter turns each rated quality into its own addressable assertion.

# data/raw/example_tasting.csv — one row
P01,yes,25-39,no,no,strong,C,3,90,28,58,74,41,3,yes

# becomes, among others:
ex:assertion/P01/C/bitterness
    a                      hto:0000005   # taste percept assertion
    hto:0000052  hto:0000114 ;             # asserts quality: bitterness
    hto:0000053  ex:rating/P01/C/bitterness .

ex:rating/P01/C/bitterness
    hto:0000070  74 ;                      # rating value
    hto:0000054  hto:0000300 .            # on the gLMS

ex:event/P01/C
    hto:0000057  hto:0000014 ;            # taster is a PROP super-taster
    hto:0000076  90 .                      # minutes since opening

Because the assertion is a thing in its own right, you can ask which people disagreed, or whether bitterness tracks taster status, without flattening anyone's rating into an average first.

Synthetic. Samples A and B were rated at 5 minutes, sample C at 90 minutes.

Read that second figure carefully. It compares three different samples that happen to have been rated at different elapsed times. It is not a within-sample time series: no sample is ever rated twice at two different times. Testing delayed bitterness properly, the phenomenon where limonin forms after juicing and a glass turns bitter as it stands, needs a repeated-measures sheet that pours once and tastes again later. HTO has the term for it, hto:0000154 delayed bitterness; the example data does not demonstrate it.

05 — LITERATURE DATA

Where it falls short

The second dataset re-annotates a published study, to show the schema fits literature and not only sheets we write ourselves. It cites a real, verified paper: Aoki and colleagues, 2023, in Nutrients, measuring PROP bitter-taste thresholds by TAS2R38 diplotype in adults in Japan.

Zero threshold values are published in this dataset, and that is a failure, not a feature. The paper reports its per-genotype thresholds only inside two figures, drawn on a log scale as images. The compute node could not fetch them, and neither could I through two other routes. Rather than read numbers off an unreadable plot, the row ships empty with a note recording exactly what is known: real sample sizes, the real ANOVA result, and the paper's own statement that non-tasters separate around 0.3 to 0.6 mM.

The consequence is that hto:0000009 detection threshold datum is exercised by the schema but by no real value. Anyone with PDF access can fill one column and complete it.

06 — TRUST

Ten of eleven citations were fabricated

The implementation plan for this ontology was drafted with citations written from memory. Every one was checked against PubMed before it entered a definition. Ten of the eleven turned out to be real identifiers pointing at completely unrelated papers: soil contaminant chemistry, plant flooding physiology, lymphoma fertility, B-lymphocyte signalling.

Only one survived, and it was the right one: the 2003 Science paper that positionally cloned TAS2R38 as the locus behind phenylthiocarbamide sensitivity. The replacements were found by search and confirmed by title and abstract before use, including the paper that coined oleogustus and the study showing humans taste glucose oligomers independently of the sweet receptor.

Every citation now in the ontology resolves and supports the statement it is attached to. The plan document still contains the bad identifiers as historical text, which is why the repository says so in docs/findings.md rather than quietly deleting them.

07 — USING IT

Build it yourself

bash scripts/setup.sh      # fetch the ROBOT jar
make build                 # templates → reason → hto.owl / .obo / .json
make report                # OBO quality checks, must be 0 errors
make validate              # 8 competency questions + 2 QC queries
python3 -m pytest tests/   # 27 tests

The competency questions are the real specification. They ask things like which qualities were reported for a given citrus juice, whether bitterness ratings differ by taster status, which tasters disagreed most about the same sample, and which participants carry a confounder that should exclude them from a bitterness analysis. If one returns nothing, the ontology has failed at a job it was built for, and make validate exits non-zero.

Known limits, stated plainly

  • The HTO prefix is not registered with the OBO Foundry, so its PURLs do not resolve yet.
  • Imports carry each external term with its label and definition, not its full ancestor chain. This is MIREOT-style, not a full extraction.
  • Chemesthetic qualities are included because panels record them, but flagged as trigeminal rather than gustatory.
  • Contested tastes sit under a separate parent, so using HTO does not commit you to calling kokumi or oleogustus a basic taste.
  • Descriptor terms are English and culture-bound. The lexicon makes no claim to cross-cultural completeness.
  • Two term requests are drafted for upstream: the four missing basic tastes to PATO, and iyokan to FoodOn, which has satsuma, yuzu and pomelo but not the citrus this city is known for.
